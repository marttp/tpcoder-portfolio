## Why This Post Exists

Have you ever joined a project and asked why the team uses a certain database, queue, or API style?

Sometimes the answer is short: **We need scale.** Or perhaps: **This is our company standard.** You may understand how to use the tool, but you still do not know why it fits this system.

If you are still improving as an engineer, this can feel uncomfortable. You may think the question is too basic. You may also think that senior engineers already know all the answers.

I do not see engineering judgment that way. **Engineering judgment starts with asking better questions, not knowing every answer.** You do not need to own the final decision before you can learn from it.

In backend and payment work, a technology choice rarely stays inside the code. The team must pay for it, run it, fix it, and teach the next engineer. This is why a technical choice can also become a business choice.

This post shares a simple way to ask why a choice was made, what it helped, what it cost, and when the team should review it again. It is not a ranking of technologies. It is a way to learn the reason behind the code you work with every day.

## Start with Four Questions

Most technologies were created to solve a problem. But when the tool becomes popular, we often remember only one benefit: fast, scalable, type-safe, or cloud-native.

You can slow the conversation down with four questions:

1. **What problem did we have?**
2. **Why did this option help?**
3. **What did we gain, and what did it cost?**
4. **Is the choice still worth it today?**

You do not need to answer all four questions alone. Asking them can help a senior engineer, product manager, or teammate share the missing context.

Consider Protobuf. A team may say that it is faster and smaller than JSON. That can be useful, but it may not be the main reason for using it. Protobuf also gives the team:

- a shared contract between services;
- generated code for different languages;
- rules for changing a schema over time.

If several teams maintain services in different languages, those benefits may matter more than speed. If one small team owns one simple service, the extra rules may add work without enough benefit.

The question is not whether Protobuf is good. The question is whether its design matches the problem and the team.

Experience from another company can help, but it is not the full answer. That company may have had more traffic, a platform team, or different security rules. We still need to check our current situation.

## From Technical Choice to Business Consequence

A better technical number does not always create a better result for the customer.

Suppose one data store reduces an operation from 50 milliseconds to 10 milliseconds. Before choosing it, ask where that operation happens.

- **If the customer waits for it:** the change may improve the customer experience.
- **If it limits system capacity:** the change may help the system handle more work.
- **If it runs in the background:** the customer may not notice the difference.
- **If it adds more work to run the system:** the team may pay more than it gains.

The new data store may also need another backup process, new monitoring, and new skills in the team. This does not make the choice bad. It means the speed and the extra work belong in the same discussion.

When you compare options, check four areas:

- **Delivery:** Which option helps the team ship safely and soon?
- **Customer or business:** What result may change?
- **Ownership:** Who will run, secure, and fix it?
- **Change later:** How hard will it be to replace?

Engineering explains these effects. The business shares its goals and limits. Then both sides can choose a cost and risk they can accept.

Sometimes the advanced option is worth it. Sometimes a tool the team already knows is the better choice. Familiar technology can leave more time for the product, especially when it can handle the expected work.

## One Platform, Two Independent Decisions

I can explain this with two projects from my time at Japan's largest cashless payment service. I chose these examples because I led both projects and was the PIC, or person in charge. I also formed the small team that worked on each project and moved it toward delivery.

Both projects were built on top of the Growth Platform. The timeline notification system came first and later made the secure-room feature possible, but each project solved a different problem.

That difference matters. Two projects can share the same platform without sharing the same reason.

### Decision 1: Remove the Weekend Manual Work

Before the timeline notification system, the marketing team had a manual process:

1. Query BigQuery for the target audience.
2. Upload the audience and notification message to a backoffice system.
3. Let the backoffice service put the work into the Growth Communication queue.
4. Ask volunteers to run the operation during weekends or holidays.

The team could send notifications this way. The problem was that people had to spend their holidays doing work that the platform should schedule for them.

We wanted the marketing team to prepare the work on a weekday, select an audience from Growth Marketing, and schedule it for a weekday, weekend, or later week. They also needed to combine audiences with conditions. The system still had to send to the right people and remain available when a scheduled job started.

There were many possible tools. We looked at Akka, normal Spring Boot, Spring Scheduler, JobRunr, RabbitMQ, and SQS. Cadence, Kafka, and Spring WebFlux were not the only tools that could work.

The strongest reason for our choice was **architecture alignment**:

- **Cadence** already existed in the Growth Platform. It was hard to set up, but once it was running well, we could rely on its workflow state and recovery.
- **Kafka** matched the existing push and email architecture. RabbitMQ or SQS would add another technology for the team to learn and run.
- **Spring WebFlux** matched the push-notification side. It also supported **Backpressure**. This means a consumer can control how much work it is ready to receive, instead of letting a producer keep sending work without a limit. We used this idea with configurable chunks, delays, and Kafka batch sizes to control throughput.
- **Moving away from Akka** also reduced future license concerns after its commercial-license change.

We still accepted a cost. The Growth Platform became a long-term owner of Cadence even though few other parts of the company used it. We also added more services. The first channel coordinator later grew into separate email, push, and timeline delivery workers, with room to split the work into smaller parts when needed.

Was it worth it? For that company and architecture, I think so. The new flow reduced around 100 hours of manual work each month, removed the need for weekend volunteers, and supported work linked to around 4 million JPY in annual GMV. The main worker also had fewer incidents than I expected. The problems I remember were more often related to human mistakes or the database than the delivery worker itself.

Would I build the same architecture in every company? No. I would first look at the tools and skills that already exist there. The implementation may change, but I would keep the same goals: schedule the work, send to the right audience, and stay available when the job runs.

### Decision 2: Add a Secure Mode for Customer Support

The second project started from another business process. Customer Support sometimes needed to handle customer data, including PII and other sensitive information. The team performed this work inside a secure room with both physical and technical access controls. They used SMS and personal calls to contact customers about suspicious activity. Some cases needed several people, which made the operation costly.

The Growth Marketing platform already had most of the useful parts:

- audience selection;
- scheduled communication;
- delivery through existing channels;
- stored history for later checks.

Instead of creating a separate product, we extended the campaign platform with two modes: normal and secure. For secure access, the infrastructure used CloudFront, the secure room's fixed IP address, and a trusted header added after the access check. We stored non-sensitive history in the database so operations, security, and audit teams could review past activity.

This choice improved the operation and removed costly SMS work, saving around 3 million JPY per year. It also kept the communication process inside a platform the business already used.

I still see a trade-off in that decision. One platform had to support two security modes. If I designed it again today, I would consider copying the full structure into a separate cloud profile. That option would cost more and take longer, but it would give the secure flow stronger isolation.

This does not mean the earlier decision was wrong. It was worth it in its context, and I can still prefer a different trade-off today.

### What These Two Decisions Taught Me

A modern system can give us many reasons to choose one option over another. Those reasons do not have the same weight:

- **Hard requirements** remove options that cannot meet security, audience, scheduling, or availability needs.
- **Architecture alignment** favors tools the team already knows, runs, and can support together.
- **Accepted trade-offs** show what extra services, dependencies, cost, or risk the team agrees to own.

The winning option is rarely the tool with the longest feature list. In these two projects, the main reason was how well the choice fit the existing platform and business process. If I started from an empty system, I might choose different tools. The overall shape would probably remain similar.

## A Mentor Should Make the Question Safe

Learning judgment should not feel like an exam. A less-experienced engineer may ask a small question because that is the only missing part they can see.

A conversation could start like this:

> **Engineer:** Why do we use Protobuf here? Is it because it is faster?
>
> **Mentor:** Speed is one benefit. What else do you think our services need to share?
>
> **Engineer:** The request and response format?
>
> **Mentor:** Yes. Now let us look at how many services and languages use that contract.

The mentor does not need to give a lecture. The mentor can:

- start from what the engineer already understands;
- add one missing part of the context;
- connect the answer to the current system;
- invite the next question;
- admit when the original reason is unknown.

That last point matters. A past decision is not bad just because the team cannot remember every detail. The team may have made a reasonable choice with the information it had. The goal is to recover the context before judging the people.

## AI Changes the Cost Equation

AI can make some parts of software work cheaper and faster:

- comparing a few approaches;
- writing repeated code;
- creating a first draft of documentation and tests.

But the team still owns the result:

- checking the domain rules and security;
- deployment, monitoring, and incident recovery;
- maintenance after the first version.

In a payment system, code that looks correct is not enough. The team must still check retry behavior, idempotency, reconciliation, access control, and failure recovery. AI can help with the work. It does not take responsibility for the result.

This may change when a team decides to build an internal tool. A small tool may now be cheap enough to try. But the same four questions still apply:

- What problem does it solve?
- What became cheaper?
- What work does the team still own?
- Is it still worth owning?

Three services created quickly are still three services to deploy, secure, and debug. Creating software may become cheaper. Understanding and owning it still matter.

## How Your Questions Can Grow

You do not need a senior title to practice engineering judgment. Start with the part of the system you already touch.

Your questions can grow step by step:

1. **Implement** — build it and understand how the chosen approach works.
2. **Explain** — describe why the design exists, including its limits and weak points.
3. **Compare** — show other options and explain what would change.
4. **Decide** — connect the technical trade-offs to delivery, risk, cost, and business needs.
5. **Own** — watch the result and review the choice when the situation changes.

You do not need to jump from the first step to the last one. You also do not need to make the final decision alone.

- An entry-level engineer can ask why the current design exists.
- A mid-level engineer can compare options with support from the team.
- A senior engineer can connect the choice to other teams and business needs.
- A mentor can help each person take the next step safely.

This path does not require becoming a people manager. An individual contributor can improve decisions, explain trade-offs, and help others work safely.

To practice, choose one existing decision without judging it first. Find out:

- what problem and limits the team had at the time;
- which other options the team considered;
- why the team chose this option;
- what extra work the team had afterward.

If nobody can answer, you found a useful gap. Record the next decision so the next engineer has a better starting point.

## A Lightweight ADR You Can Revisit

You can write this context in an **Architecture Decision Record (ADR)**. An ADR does not need to be a long or formal document. A small decision can have a small record.

For many decisions, I would start with something this small:

```text
# Decision: [Short name]

Date: [When we made it]
Status: [Proposed / Accepted / Replaced]

Problem:
Business goal:
Constraints:
Technology intention:

Options considered:
Decisive reasons:
Decision:
Trade-offs accepted:

Owner:
Reversal cost:
Revisit when:
```

Three fields deserve special attention:

- **Options considered:** Keep the options the team rejected and why. The next engineer will not need to repeat all the work.
- **Date and constraints:** A good choice can become a poor fit when traffic, rules, team size, cost, or the product changes.
- **Revisit when:** Add a clear signal for reviewing the choice, such as more traffic, another team using the contract, or a higher vendor cost.

An ADR cannot remove uncertainty or missing information. It can help the team see the reason, cost, and owner of a decision. It also gives a less-experienced engineer a safe place to learn why the system looks this way.

Engineering judgment does not mean choosing the best answer on the first attempt. It means asking useful questions, making the current reason visible, and checking the choice again when the situation changes.

If you are still improving, you do not need to wait for a new title. Pick one decision in your current project and ask why. That is already practice.
